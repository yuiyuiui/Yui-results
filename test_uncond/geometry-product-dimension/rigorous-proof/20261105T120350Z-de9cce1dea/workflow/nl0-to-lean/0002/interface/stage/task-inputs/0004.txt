Basis proof for the complete selected target. All Hausdorff measures below are unnormalized diameter measures: no dimensional prefactor, ball-radius convention, probability normalization, or new hypothesis is introduced. The only H is the original one: n is an arbitrary natural number and E⊆Euclidean ℝ^n is nonempty and compact. Write X=Rn(n), I=[0,1], F=E×I, and D=dim_H E. We use the maximum metric on X×ℝ during the covering argument and return to the required Euclidean metric in paragraph 8. The original proof route is replaced only as recorded in the replacements array. The B statements are dependencies with proofs/reductions, not a permission list restricting the freely available pinned Mathlib.

1. Content and normalization (B101 from B001). For a>0 and δ>0 let C^a_δ(A) be the infimum of Σ_i(diam U_i)^a over countable δ-covers of A. In Lean this is BasisObjects.content a (ENNReal.ofReal δ) A. A set with extended diameter ≤δ<∞ is bounded, and its extended diameter is the cast of its real diameter. Empty sets contribute zero: the header explicitly uses the supremum over a nonemptiness proof, which is zero when empty and is the unique displayed value when nonempty. This also agrees with 0^a=0 for a>0. A finite cover may be padded by empty sets. A cover with any countable index type can be enumerated with empty padding after an injection into ℕ, and conversely an ℕ-family is countable. The sums are unchanged. Thus these contents coincide exactly with those in the original problem. No normalizing constant occurs.

The admissible covers for δ₁≤δ₂ form nested families, giving C^a_δ₂≤C^a_δ₁. Each admissible cover bounds the infimum above. A bound from below for every admissible cover bounds its infimum below, also when there are no admissible covers (infimum of the empty family is ∞). B001 identifies H^a with the supremum of these contents over positive extended scales. Scales ∞ add nothing: content at ∞ is at most the content at any fixed finite positive scale. Every finite positive extended scale is the cast of a positive real scale. Hence H^a(A)=sup_{δ>0} C^a_δ(A), exactly the problem's convention. In particular every finite-scale content is ≤H^a.

2. Approximation of a zero content (completion of B101). In the complete lattice ℝ≥0∞, if the infimum of a family is zero then for every finite ε>0 some family member is <ε; otherwise ε is a positive lower bound. Apply this to the nested infima over U and the cover and diameter conditions. An unsuccessful condition has empty infimum and value ∞, so a value <ε provides an actual admissible U. Conversely covers of arbitrarily small positive cost make the content zero. Taking the supremum over δ proves: H^a(A)=0 iff at every positive scale there are countable covers of arbitrarily small positive a-cost. All costs and sums are interpreted in ℝ≥0∞; finite estimates can equivalently be written in nonnegative reals.

3. Upper cover construction (B102). Let X be any metric space, A⊆X, q>0 and H^q(A)=0. Fix a target scale δ>0 and ε>0. Put r=min(1,δ)>0. By paragraph 2 choose a countable r-cover U_i with Σ d_i^q<ε/4, where d_i=diam U_i (zero for empty sets). Choose numbers 0<a_i≤r with a_i^q≤ε 2^(-i-3). For example take the minimum of r and (ε 2^(-i-3))^(1/q); all quantities are positive. Then Σ a_i^q≤ε/4, since Σ_{i≥0}2^(-i-3)=1/4. Put r_i=max(d_i,a_i). These numbers satisfy 0<r_i≤r≤1, and r_i^q=max(d_i^q,a_i^q)≤d_i^q+a_i^q. Summing gives Σ r_i^q<ε/2. This numerical padding handles zero-diameter singletons in every metric space, including X=ℝ^0; no replacement ball or additional ambient point is required.

Let N_i=ceil(1/r_i), a positive integer. The intervals [j/N_i,(j+1)/N_i], 0≤j<N_i, cover I and have length 1/N_i≤r_i. Also N_i≤1/r_i+1≤2/r_i. The products U_i×[j/N_i,(j+1)/N_i] cover A×I. Their diameters in the maximum metric are at most r_i, because both coordinate distances are at most r_i. Empty products have cost zero. The index set {(i,j):i∈ℕ, j<N_i} is countable, so this is an admissible countable cover at scale δ. Its total (q+1)-cost is at most Σ_i N_i r_i^(q+1)≤2 Σ_i r_i^q<ε. The equality r_i^(q+1)/r_i=r_i^q is legitimate since every r_i>0. As δ and ε were arbitrary, paragraph 2 gives H^(q+1)(A×I)=0.

4. Lower slicing construction (B103). Let X be any metric space, A⊆X, s>0 and δ>0. Set c=C^s_δ(A), with c allowed to be ∞. Take any countable δ-cover V_i of A×I in the maximum product metric. For a nonempty V_i let ρ_i=diam V_i, A_i=π_X(V_i), and B_i=π_ℝ(V_i). Coordinate distances do not exceed product distances, so diam A_i≤ρ_i≤δ and diam B_i≤ρ_i. A nonempty B_i is bounded above and below, because it has finite diameter; define J_i=[inf B_i,sup B_i]. The defining least-upper-bound and greatest-lower-bound inequalities show B_i⊆J_i. They also show sup B_i-inf B_i≤ρ_i: every b-b' with b,b'∈B_i is ≤ρ_i, then take the supremum in b and infimum in b'. Consequently J_i is a measurable closed interval and B007 gives volume J_i≤ρ_i. For empty V_i put ρ_i=0, A_i=B_i=J_i=∅; all these conclusions still hold. Notice that this does not assert measurability of V_i, A_i or B_i.

For any t∈I, the family consisting of A_i when t∈J_i and ∅ otherwise is a δ-cover of A. Indeed (x,t) lies in some V_i whenever x∈A, and then x∈A_i and t∈B_i⊆J_i. Therefore
c≤Σ_i ρ_i^s 1_{J_i}(t) for every t∈I.
Equivalently, on all of ℝ,
1_I(t)c ≤ Σ_i 1_{I∩J_i}(t)ρ_i^s.
Outside I both sides are zero; this also works for c=∞ because indicator functions use a zero branch, avoiding any ambiguous real multiplication. Every I∩J_i is measurable. Integrate this inequality with Lebesgue measure. B009 gives monotonicity, B010 evaluates the indicators, B007 gives volume I=1, and B008 exchanges the nonnegative series and lower integral. The result is
c=c·volume I
 ≤Σ_i ρ_i^s volume(I∩J_i)
 ≤Σ_i ρ_i^s volume J_i
 ≤Σ_i ρ_i^s ρ_i
 =Σ_i ρ_i^(s+1).
Indicator summands are measurable, hence AEMeasurable as required by B008. The second inequality uses inclusion and the monotonicity of a measure. All powers are nonnegative; ρ_i is finite; the power identity holds also for ρ_i=0 because s>0. These inequalities hold in ℝ≥0∞, and do not require c or the series to be finite. Taking the infimum over the arbitrary covers gives C^s_δ(A)≤C^(s+1)_δ(A×I). Taking suprema over δ and using paragraph 1 gives H^s(A)≤H^(s+1)(A×I).

5. Finite dimensions and the fibre bound (part of B104). By B003 and B004,
0≤D≤dimH(X)=finrank ℝ X=n.
The rank equality follows from the n standard Euclidean coordinate vectors; for n=0 it is the empty basis. Thus D is finite and may be identified with its nonnegative real value without changing addition or order. The same argument in X×ℝ gives dimH F≤n+1<∞ in the maximum norm; its coordinate basis has n+1 vectors.

The set I has nonempty real interior: (0,1)⊆interior I and, for example, 1/2 belongs to it. B012 with finrank ℝ ℝ=1 gives dimH I=1. Choose x₀∈E using the original nonemptiness assumption. The map t↦(x₀,t) is an isometry for the maximum product metric, since max(dist(x₀,x₀),|t-u|)=|t-u|. B006 and B003 therefore give dimH F≥dimH({x₀}×I)=1. This also proves the entire required lower bound when D=0.

6. Upper dimension inequality (part of B104). For any real q>D, we have q>0. Regard q as a nonnegative real when invoking B002. Its critical-exponent zero conclusion gives H^q(E)=0. Paragraph 3 gives H^(q+1)(F)=0, so this measure is not ∞ and B002 gives dimH F≤q+1. This holds for every q>D. Because D and dimH F are finite, it implies dimH F≤D+1: if dimH F>D+1, choose q strictly between D and dimH F-1, contradicting dimH F≤q+1. No assumption n≥1 has been made, so this includes n=0.

7. Lower dimension inequality (completion of B104). If D>0, choose any real s with 0<s<D. B002 gives H^s(E)=∞. Paragraph 4 gives H^(s+1)(F)≥H^s(E)=∞, so H^(s+1)(F)=∞. B002 now yields dimH F≥s+1. Together with the fibre bound this implies dimH F≥D+1: if instead dimH F<D+1, the fibre bound gives dimH F-1≥0, and one can choose s strictly between dimH F-1 and D, a contradiction. If D=0, paragraph 5 already gives dimH F≥D+1. Combined with paragraph 6 this proves the maximum-norm equality for every n and the original nonempty compact E. Compactness is retained as H even though the replacement cover argument needs no further use of it.

8. Return to the specified Euclidean target (B105). The header defines euclideanLift n as the identity on pairs into WithLp 2 (Rn(n)×ℝ). The prepared adapter B011 is an actual closed Lean proof from B005 using the inverse of WithLp.prodContinuousLinearEquiv 2 ℝ (Rn n) ℝ. Its underlying function is exactly euclideanLift, and it proves that every image set has the same Hausdorff dimension as in the maximum norm. This is an explicit metric-change justification, valid for all n≥0 and all sets.

B013 shows that the norm in this new space satisfies ‖(x,t)‖²=‖x‖²+|t|². Apply it to differences. In Rn(n), the squared Euclidean norm is Σ_{j<n}|x_j|² by the defining Euclidean norm. Consequently coordinate concatenation j(x,t)=(x₀,…,x_(n-1),t) has
dist(j(x,t),j(y,u))²=Σ_{j<n}|x_j-y_j|²+|t-u|²
=dist(euclideanLift(x,t),euclideanLift(y,u))².
Distances are nonnegative, so equality of squares gives equality of distances. This proves an isometry into Euclidean ℝ^(n+1); truncation of the first n coordinates and extraction of the last coordinate give its inverse. For n=0 the sum is empty and the same construction is the usual identification of ℝ^0×ℝ with ℝ. It sends euclideanLift '' (E×I) exactly to the original Euclidean product set. B006 preserves its dimension. Applying B011 and paragraph 7 therefore gives, in precisely the selected Euclidean metric,
dim_H(E×[0,1])=dim_H E+1.
The right side is the original dimension of E in the Euclidean metric of ℝ^n. All dimensions here are finite, so the ℝ≥0∞ equality is the same equality of real dimensions in the original statement. This completes the full target without residual textbook premises.
